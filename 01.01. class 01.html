1.1 Welcome

Good morning, and welcome to CS 330 – Software Engineering. My name is on the board; you can call me Cyber. Before I came into the classroom I spent about twenty-five years in the IT (Information Technology) industry, building and maintaining systems for people who needed them to work on Monday morning. That experience is the reason this course is organised the way it is: you will not learn software engineering by listening to me for three hours; you will learn it by building one small product, as a team, from the first idea to a released version, and by doing every week exactly what a professional team does in a week.

Let us start with names. In a moment I will ask every one of you three things: your name, your background, and one thing you have built — a web page, a script, a game level, a spreadsheet with formulas, anything. There is no wrong answer; I ask because the four people at your table next month will need to know what each of you can already do.

Slide 4

1.2 What this course is about

The syllabus defines our subject in one sentence: software engineering is the engineering discipline whose aim is the production of software products that are delivered on time, within an agreed budget, and that satisfy the needs of the client. Read that sentence again and notice three measurable things in it: time, budget and client needs. A program that works but was delivered a year late, at three times the price, and does not do what the user asked for, is a working program and a failed engineering project. Keeping those three things under control, at the same time, is the whole subject.

The course has two halves. In the first half we study how professionals organise the work: the software process, the process models, process improvement, and agile development. In the second half we walk through the phases of building a product — requirements, analysis, design, coding, testing and delivery — and the activities that support all of them: configuration management, quality assurance, documentation and maintenance. Throughout, we return to the professional, ethical and social responsibility of the engineer, because software today decides who gets paid, who gets treated, and who gets home safely.

Slide 5

The University describes five LO (Learning Outcome)s for this course. In plain words: (1) you will be able to argue for and against the industry standards for software processes; (2) you will apply design patterns and framework activities to a design; (3) you will characterise the five types of testing — unit, integration, system, user acceptance and performance; (4) you will analyse and present continuous delivery; and (5) you will recognise your professional, ethical and social responsibility. Our textbook is Pressman and Maxim, Software Engineering: A Practitioner's Approach, ninth edition, 2020 — the chapter numbers I give you every week refer to that edition.

1.3 How a week works: the 1:2:2 rhythm

Every Tuesday has the same shape, and I want you to memorise it today. One hour of lecture, two hours of studio, two hours of work at home. We write it as 1:2:2: one part lecture, two parts Studio, two parts Home Sprint. Concretely:

  • 0:00–1:00 — Lecture in three or four parts. I speak for fifteen minutes at most, then we stop for a five-minute Active Break: you sketch, you try something on your own product, you quiz a neighbour with flash-cards, you answer three quick questions, or you sit in silence for two minutes. Research on learning is clear on one point: retrieving an idea from your own memory fixes it far better than hearing it a second time. The breaks are not decoration; they are where the lecture becomes knowledge.
  • 1:00–1:10 — Break. Ten real minutes.
  • 1:10–2:50 — Studio (100 minutes). You sit with your team and produce this week's Block while I circulate from table to table. I do not lecture in the Studio; I ask questions and I unblock you.
  • 2:50–3:00 — Wrap-up. The Block box goes on the screen, I say the due date aloud, and you hand me an exit ticket — one sentence on paper answering one question.
  • At home — Home Sprint (120 minutes). You finish the Block, read the assigned chapters, review your flash-cards and do the Numbers practice when there is one.

1.4 Blocks, the Binder, the Mid-Term and the Release

A Block is the concrete work product your team produces in one week: a document, a diagram, a plan, code with tests, a release. There are fourteen Blocks over fifteen Tuesdays. Each Block is due on the Sunday after the class that produces it, at 23:59, course local time, as one submission per team on the LMS (Learning Management System): the link to your team repository and a Git tag named after the Block. Every Block reuses the previous one: the vision you write today is the object of the ethics brief next week, of the process decision the week after, of the backlog, the requirements, the design, the code and the tests. Nothing you make in this course is thrown away.

Slide 9

The Blocks accumulate into your Binder — the integrated project binder, the single consolidated document of everything your team has decided and built. Binder version 1 (Block 8) is due on Sunday, November 15. Two days later, on Tuesday, November 17, you sit the Mid-Term. Here is the sentence I will repeat all semester: the Mid-Term is Block 9 of your project. It is worth thirty percent of your grade, it is closed book, and its application questions ask you to do, on a product the size of yours, exactly what Blocks 3 to 7 made you do: choose and justify a process model, size and plan the work, write requirements and a use case, pick a design pattern, design test cases and compute complexity and coverage. No notes are allowed in the room. Your only notes are what you internalised by building your Blocks on time. A late or missing Block is a hole in your preparation, and the exam will find it.

After the Mid-Term comes Sprint 2: the integration and pipeline Block on November 24, then three supervised Integration Labs in December — Lab 1 Build it (December 1), Lab 2 Check it (December 8) and Lab 3 Ship it (December 15). On December 15 you tag Release 1.0 of your product. The winter break runs from December 21 to January 8. On January 12 you present your product at the Showcase and we review for the Final; on January 19 you sit the Final, worth forty percent, covering all five outcomes.

Slide 16
2.1 A definition with three parts
Ask most people what software is and they answer “programs”. That answer is one third of the truth. In engineering terms, software is the combination of three things: (1) programs — instructions that, when executed by a computer, provide the required function and performance; (2) data structures — the organised information that those programs read, store and change; and (3) documentation — the descriptive information, on paper or on screen, that explains what the programs do and how to use and operate them. This is the definition used by Pressman and by the SWEBOK (Software Engineering Body of Knowledge) published by the IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) Computer Society, and it is the definition we use in this course.
Take a concrete product from our menu: ClubLend, the application a student club uses to lend equipment. The programs are the code that shows the catalogue, registers a loan, and sends an overdue reminder. The data are the catalogue of items, the list of members, and the loan records — with their structure: which fields exist, which values are allowed, how a loan refers to an item and a member. The documentation is the README (the 'read me' file of a repository) that explains how to install the application, the user guide that explains how to borrow a projector, and the rule that says a loan lasts at most seven days. Delete any one of the three and the product stops being usable: code without data does nothing useful; data without code is a spreadsheet; both without documentation cannot be operated by anyone except the person who wrote them, and only until that person forgets.
Why insist on this? Because in this course two of your fourteen Blocks are documentation (the charter and vision this week, the user guide in December), one is a data model (Block 5), and the graders of your Binder treat all three parts as equally real work products. Professionals do the same.
Slide 17
2.2 Three characteristics that make software different
  • Software is developed or engineered; it is not manufactured. Once a program is correct, copying it costs nothing and introduces no defects. All the cost and all the risk are in the development, not in the production line. This is why the process of development — our subject — matters so much.
  • Software does not wear out. A mechanical part fails more often as it ages because its material degrades. Software has no material. Its failure rate, however, does rise over time, for a different reason: every change made to it — a new feature, a fix, an adaptation to a new OS (Operating System) — can introduce new defects. We say software deteriorates through change, and that is why we test again after every change (you will do exactly that in Lab 3, with a regression test after a change request).
  • Most software is still custom-built, even though component-based construction — assembling a product from reusable parts such as libraries and frameworks — keeps growing. Your product will reuse a web framework and libraries; the part that makes it StudyBuddy or ClubLend is still written by you.
Slides 18–19
2.3 The categories of software
Pressman groups today's software into seven application domains. Knowing the category matters because each has different quality demands, different risks, and therefore a different process — which is the decision you will make in Block 3.
  • System software — programs written to serve other programs: operating systems, compilers, file managers, drivers. Heavy interaction with the hardware; the OS (Operating System) on your laptop is the example you use every day.
  • Application software — stand-alone programs that solve a specific need of a person or a business: a word processor, a payroll system, a library loan system such as ClubLend.
  • Engineering and scientific software — programs whose core is calculation or simulation: weather models, computer-aided design, structural analysis.
  • Embedded software — software that lives inside a product and controls its functions for the user and for the product itself: the firmware in a barcode scanner, the controller of a washing machine, the flight software of a rocket. It often runs with strict timing constraints.
  • Product-line software — software built to be sold to many different customers with the same core: an inventory package, a spreadsheet, a computer game engine.
  • Web and mobile applications — browser-based and phone-based applications backed by servers and databases; all five products on our menu are in this category.
  • AI (Artificial Intelligence) software — programs that use non-numerical algorithms, statistical models or learned models to solve problems: recommendation systems, image recognition, language models.
Slide 19
One more term you will meet in industry on your first day: legacy software. It is old software — often decades old — that still runs a business-critical process and that is difficult to change, because its documentation is missing, its authors have left, or its technology is no longer taught. The Government of Canada's previous pay system, which Phoenix was meant to replace, was forty years old. Legacy is not an insult; it is a category with its own engineering rules, and the first rule is: document before you touch.
Slide 20
2.4 Why the category decides the engineering
Compare two programs with the same size: a mobile application that suggests study partners, and the embedded controller of an insulin pump. A defect in the first annoys a student; a defect in the second can kill a patient. The second program therefore gets formal reviews, exhaustive testing, certification and a slow, documented change procedure; the first can be released every week. Same size, different category, different process. When you choose your process model in Block 3, the first question you will answer is: what category is our product, and what happens when it fails?

Lesson text
Slide 23
3.1 Programming and engineering are different activities
Programming is the activity of writing instructions that a computer executes correctly. It is a genuine skill, and most of you already have some of it. But look at what a programmer working alone controls: the code. Now look at what a client pays for: a product that arrives on a date, at a price, does what was agreed, keeps working after the programmer has left, and can be changed safely next year. Those are different objects. The gap between the two is what engineering fills.
Slide 24
The IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) gave the standard definition in its glossary of software engineering terminology (IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) Std 610.12-1990, now carried into the ISO (International Organization for Standardization)/IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission)/IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) 24765 vocabulary): software engineering is the application of a systematic, disciplined, quantifiable approach to the development, operation and maintenance of software; that is, the application of engineering to software. Three adjectives carry the whole definition. Systematic: there is a defined sequence of activities, not improvisation. Disciplined: the sequence is followed even under pressure — especially under pressure. Quantifiable: size, effort, defects, coverage and schedule are measured with numbers, so that decisions rest on evidence rather than on optimism. Notice also the three nouns: development, operation and maintenance. Engineering does not stop when the code compiles; it covers the years the software is used and changed.
Engineering adds three things to programming. Process: who does what, when, and what they hand over. Quality: defined, measured and reviewed, not assumed. Accountability: a named person or team answers for the result to identifiable stakeholders — the client who pays, the users who depend on it, the operators who run it, and the public who may be affected by it. A student writing a script alone to rename files is programming; the same student, in a team of four, producing a documented, tested, released version of ClubLend for a real club, is engineering.
Slide 25
3.2 The cost of change
Here is the single most important number-shaped idea of today. Every project changes its mind: a requirement is misunderstood, a user asks for one more field, a law changes. The cost of change is the effort needed to make one change to the product — and it is not constant. It grows with the phase of the project in which the change is made. Barry Boehm's studies of large projects in the 1970s and 1980s found that correcting a requirements error after release could cost tens to a hundred times more than correcting it while the requirements were being written. The exact multiplier depends on the domain; the shape of the curve is the same everywhere.
Slide 26
The reason is not mysterious. A decision taken early is copied into everything built afterwards. Change a requirement during requirements, and you edit one sentence. Change it during design, and you also redraw the models that depended on it. Change it during construction, and you also rewrite code and unit tests. Change it after release, and you also re-test the whole product for regressions — defects introduced by the change into features that used to work — you redeploy to every installation, you retrain the users, and you migrate the data already stored in the old form. Each phase adds a layer of dependent work products, and the change must propagate through all of them.
Two consequences for you. First, the cheapest defect is the one found in a review of a document, which is why this course has Blocks of requirements and design before Blocks of code, and why professionals review documents formally. Second, agile methods — Class 4 — exist largely to keep the curve flat by keeping the batches of change small and the feedback fast. In Lab 3, in December, your team will receive a real change request against your released product, and you will measure the difference yourselves.

3.3 Why software projects fail: three documented cases
Failures are studied, not hidden; every one of the cases below has a public inquiry report, and I link the official documents so that you can read the primary source rather than a retelling.
  • Ariane 5, flight 501 — June 4, 1996. Thirty-seven seconds after the main engine ignition sequence started, the new European launcher veered off course, broke up, and was destroyed by its own safety system, together with the four Cluster scientific satellites it carried. The ESA (European Space Agency)–CNES (Centre national d'études spatiales, the French space agency) inquiry board found the cause in the software of the SRI (Système de Référence Inertielle, the inertial reference system): a program reused unchanged from Ariane 4 converted a 64-bit floating-point value (the horizontal velocity) to a 16-bit signed integer; on the faster Ariane 5 trajectory the value did not fit, the conversion raised an exception that was not handled, the computer shut itself down, and its identical backup shut down for the same reason a few milliseconds earlier. The reviews and tests of the development programme had not analysed or tested this unit under Ariane 5 conditions. Who paid? The agencies and the scientists whose satellites were lost — a loss usually estimated at several hundred million dollars. Read the board's own findings: ESA (European Space Agency) press release presenting the Inquiry Board report scan and the Inquiry Board report (ESA (European Space Agency) Science) scan.
  • Therac-25 — 1985 to 1987. A computer-controlled radiation-therapy machine, whose software had replaced the hardware safety interlocks of earlier models, delivered massive overdoses to at least six patients in the United States and Canada; several died. The investigation by Nancy Leveson and Clark Turner identified a race condition triggered by fast operator input, error messages nobody understood, and a manufacturer that at first insisted the software could not be at fault. We return to this case next week when we discuss the engineer's responsibility; the primary source is Leveson & Turner, “An Investigation of the Therac-25 Accidents” (MIT (Massachusetts Institute of Technology) copy) scan.
  • Phoenix — Government of Canada, 2016. Closer to home: the pay system for about 290,000 federal employees. In 2018 the OAG (Office of the Auditor General of Canada) concluded that the project was, in the Auditor General's words, an incomprehensible failure of project management and project oversight. To meet the schedule and the budget, the department had removed critical pay functions, curtailed system tests, and cancelled the pilot with one department before launching in two waves in February and April 2016. Tens of thousands of public servants were underpaid, overpaid or not paid at all; the cost of repairing the system has grown into the billions of dollars. Who paid? Employees who could not pay their rent, and every taxpayer. The primary source is the 2018 Spring Report 1 — Building and Implementing the Phoenix Pay System (OAG (Office of the Auditor General of Canada)) scan.

None of these is a failure of programming talent; all of them are failures of process, quality and accountability — the three things engineering adds. That is the reason this course exists.

4.1 Four layers, one direction of dependency
Pressman describes software engineering as a layered technology: four layers, each one resting on the layer below it, so that a layer has no value without the layers beneath it. The IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) Computer Society uses the same description (‘Software Engineering as a Layered Technology’, IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) Computer Society scan). From the bottom up:
Slide 35
  • Layer 1 — Quality focus. An organisational commitment to quality: the agreement, shared by everyone on the team, that the product will be correct, usable, maintainable and safe, and that this will be checked rather than assumed. Movements such as TQM (Total Quality Management) built a culture of continuous improvement on this commitment. Without it the other layers become theatre: a process that nobody follows when the deadline comes, methods applied for the report and not for the result.
  • Layer 2 — Process. The framework of activities, actions and tasks that must be established for the effective delivery of software. The process is the layer that management controls: it defines what work products are produced, when milestones occur, how quality is checked and how change is managed. Pressman calls it the foundation layer because methods and tools are applied inside a process; without one, they are applied at random.
  • Layer 3 — Methods. The technical ‘how to’ for each task: how to elicit requirements, how to model a system, how to design a component, how to construct a program, how to design test cases, how to support the product. Methods rest on principles and include modelling notations (the use-case diagram, the class diagram) and descriptive techniques (a requirements template, a test-case table).
  • Layer 4 — Tools. Automated or semi-automated support for the process and the methods: version control (Git scan), a hosted repository with pull requests and boards (GitHub scan), a continuous-integration service (GitHub Actions scan), a unit-test framework (pytest scan), a diagram editor. When tools are integrated so that the information produced by one is used by another, the result is called CASE (Computer-Aided Software Engineering).
Slide 36
The direction of dependency is the examinable point. Tools support methods; methods are applied within a process; the process only works when it rests on a quality focus. Read it the other way round and you have the most common mistake of new teams: choosing the tool first (‘we will use this framework and this pipeline’), then looking for a method that fits the tool, then inventing a process around both, and never asking what quality means for this product. A tool without a method is a purchase, not engineering.
Slide 37
4.2 Where each layer appears in this course
You will build all four layers yourselves, and you can already see where. The quality focus is written down in Block 2, when your ethics brief says what must never happen with your product (a report lost, a reporter exposed, a payment charged twice). The process is decided in Block 3 (your process model decision record) and lived every week through the Blocks. The methods are the content of Blocks 5, 6 and 7: elicitation and use cases, architectural styles and design patterns, black-box and white-box test-case design. The tools are set up in Block 1 (the repository) and completed in Block 10 (the configuration management plan and the first green continuous-integration pipeline). When you read your Binder v1 in November, you should be able to point at each layer in it.

4.3 A test you can apply in the Studio today

A team whose charter contains only tool decisions has built the top of the stack with nothing under it.

5.1 What a process is made of
A software process is a collection of activities, actions and tasks that are performed when a work product is to be created. Those three words are nested. A framework activity is a broad objective, such as ‘communication’. Each activity is populated by software engineering actions — a related set of tasks that produces a major work product, such as ‘requirements gathering’ or ‘architectural design’. Each action is defined by a task set: the concrete work tasks, the work products they produce, the quality-assurance points required and the milestones that mark progress. So the hierarchy is activity → action → task, and a task is the level at which one of you actually does something this afternoon.
Pressman's generic process framework — generic because it applies to every software project, from a six-week student product to a national pay system — defines five framework activities. The names are worth memorising in order; they are on your flash-cards.
Slide 41
5.2 The five framework activities
  • Communication. Collaboration with the customer and the other stakeholders to understand the objectives and gather the requirements that define the features and functions of the software. For HabitHub, it is the interview in which a student explains what ‘a habit slipped this week’ means to her.
  • Planning. Establishing the map for the work: the technical tasks, the likely risks, the resources required, the work products to be produced, and the schedule. For HabitHub, it is the sprint plan that puts ‘mark a habit done’ in Sprint 1 with an estimate of three points.
  • Modeling. Creating models — sketches, diagrams, structured text — to understand the requirements (analysis) and the design (design) before building. For HabitHub, a use case for marking a habit and a class sketch with Habit, Entry and Streak.
  • Construction. Code generation and the testing that uncovers defects in it. For HabitHub, the function that records an entry, its unit tests, and the pull request that carries both.
  • Deployment. Delivering the software, complete or as an increment, to the customer, who evaluates it and gives feedback that starts the next cycle. For HabitHub, tag v0.2, ten students use it for a week, and their feedback becomes the next stories.
Slide 42
5.3 The umbrella activities
The five activities describe what a project does in sequence. A second set, the umbrella activities, runs across all of them, all the time. Pressman lists: software project tracking and control (comparing progress with the plan and acting on the difference); risk management (identifying what may go wrong and preparing for it); SQA (Software Quality Assurance) (the activities that ensure the product meets its quality requirements); technical reviews (examining work products to find and remove errors before they propagate); measurement (collecting process, project and product metrics); SCM (Software Configuration Management) (managing the effects of change throughout the process — versions, baselines, tags); reusability management (criteria and mechanisms for reusing work products); and work product preparation and production (creating the models, documents, logs and forms themselves). Every one of these has a Block in this course: tracking is your stand-up log, risk is in your sprint plan, SQA (Software Quality Assurance) and reviews are Block 10 and the peer FTR (Formal Technical Review) of Lab 2, measurement is coverage and complexity in Block 7, and SCM (Software Configuration Management) is the tag you push every Sunday.
Slide 43
5.4 Process flow: the same five activities, arranged differently
The framework says what the activities are; the process flow says how they are arranged in time. A linear flow performs the five once, in sequence, from communication to deployment — the classical waterfall. An iterative flow repeats one or more activities before moving on. An evolutionary flow performs the five activities in a circle, each circuit producing a more complete version of the software. A parallel flow runs activities at the same time on different parts of the product. Class 3 studies the process models built on these flows; today it is enough to see that this course itself is an evolutionary flow with three circuits: Sprint 0, Sprint 1 and Sprint 2, each ending in a deployment — a Binder, a Mid-Term, a Release.

Step-by-step: how the program works

  2. An SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics) pipeline draws the five stations as boxes joined by arrows; a circle (the story token) is moved from box to box by changing its x coordinate.

  6. Restart returns the token to the start, zeroes the counters and clears the trace.
