Today's question

Good morning. In Class 1 you formed a team and chose a product. Last week you asked who that product could hurt. Today we answer the next question every team runs into: in what order will we do the work, how many times will we repeat each part, and how will we know that a part is finished? Our discipline has a name for the answer — the software process.

This hour is the first of two on LO1 (Learning Outcome 1): debating, with arguments, the industry standards for software process. Today you get the vocabulary, one standard, six models and a method for choosing among them. Next week adds agile methods and process improvement. Before you leave the Studio, your team will have chosen a process for its own product and written the choice down, with numbers.

Process, activity, action, task

Definitionsoftware process — A collection of activities, actions and tasks performed when a work product is to be created. The team adapts it: it selects the actions and tasks that suit the project, its people and its deadline.
Definitionactivity — Work aimed at a broad objective — for example, communication with stakeholders — that applies to every project, whatever its domain, size or complexity.
Definitionaction — A set of tasks that produces a major work product — for example, architectural design, which produces an architecture model.
Definitiontask — A small, well-defined piece of work with a tangible outcome — for example, running one unit test.
Definitionwork product — Anything the process produces and keeps: a document, a model, code, test data, a release.

Take QueueLess, the cafeteria pre-order app from our product menu. Modeling is an activity. Designing the order screen is an action inside it. Drawing the wireframe of the order screen and reviewing the wireframe with the cafeteria manager are two tasks inside that action. The wireframe itself is a work product.

The five framework activities

Definitionprocess framework — The foundation of a complete software process: a small set of framework activities that apply to every software project, together with the umbrella activities that apply across them.

Your textbook, Pressman and Maxim, 9th edition↗scan, names five framework activities:

  1. Communication — understand the stakeholders' objectives and gather the requirements.
  2. Planning — describe the technical tasks, the risks, the resources, the work products and the schedule. The result is the project plan.
  3. Modeling — build models of the requirements and of the design, so that the problem and its solution are understood before code is written.
  4. Construction — write the code and test it, so that errors are found while they are still cheap to correct.
  5. Deployment — deliver the software, complete or as one increment, to the users, who evaluate it and give feedback.

The same five activities exist in a two-person mobile app and in a flight-control system. What changes from one project to the next is the amount of detail inside each activity, the order of the activities and the number of times they are repeated.

Umbrella activities

Definitionumbrella activities — Activities applied throughout the whole project, across all the framework activities, to manage and control progress, quality, change and risk.

The textbook lists eight of them:

  • software project tracking and control — compare progress with the plan, and act when they differ;
  • risk management — identify what could go wrong and decide what to do about it;
  • SQA (Software Quality Assurance) — define and carry out the activities that ensure the quality of the software;
  • technical reviews — examine work products to find errors before they move on to the next activity;
  • measurement — collect measures of the process, the project and the product;
  • SCM (Software Configuration Management) — manage the effects of change on every work product;
  • reusability management — set the rules for reusing work products, and decide what to reuse;
  • work product preparation and production — produce the models, documents, logs and lists the project needs.

None of these is a phase. They run every week. You already practise four of them: the weekly stand-up is tracking and control, the harms table of Block 2 is risk management, last week's cross-team review was a technical review, and one Git tag per Block is configuration management.

Process flows

Definitionprocess flow — The way the framework activities, and the actions and tasks inside them, are organised in sequence and in time.
  • Linear process flow — each of the five activities is performed once, in sequence, ending with deployment.
  • Iterative process flow — one or more activities are repeated before the team proceeds to the next one.
  • Evolutionary process flow — the activities are performed in a circuit, and each circuit produces a more complete version of the software.
  • Parallel process flow — one or more activities are performed at the same time as others; for example, one part of the software is modeled while another part is being built.

Look at this course's calendar with these four words. Sprint 0, Sprint 1 and Sprint 2 each go around the circuit and end with something delivered: the flow of the course is evolutionary. In December, one feature is tested while another is still being coded: that part of the flow is parallel.

Process patterns

Definitionprocess pattern — A description of a process-related problem met during software work, of the environment in which the problem occurs, and of one or more proven solutions to it.

The textbook distinguishes three types:

  • a stage pattern describes a problem tied to one framework activity — for example, establishing communication with stakeholders;
  • a task pattern describes a problem tied to an action or a task — for example, requirements gathering;
  • a phase pattern describes the sequence of framework activities inside the process — for example, the spiral model or prototyping, which you will meet in twenty minutes.

Every pattern is written with the same fields: name, forces, type, initial context, problem, solution, resulting context, related patterns and known uses. Here is one for your projects. Problem: the client is rarely available, so questions wait for weeks. Initial context: the requirements are incomplete and the team works part-time. Solution: one fixed fifteen-minute check-in every week, the list of open questions sent the day before, and every decision recorded in the repository. Resulting context: questions are answered within a week, and every decision can be found later.

That is the vocabulary of a process. In four minutes you will use it on your own product.

    What a standard is, and who writes them
    A process framework tells you which kinds of work exist. A standard goes further: it fixes the words and the list of work so that two companies, or two teams, mean the same thing when they say design or maintenance.
    Definitionstandard — A document established by consensus and approved by a recognised body that provides, for common and repeated use, rules, guidelines or characteristics for activities or their results.
    Three bodies matter for us. The IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) (official site↗scan) is a professional association that publishes standards through its Standards Association↗scan. The ISO (International Organization for Standardization) (official site↗scan) and the IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission) (official site↗scan) publish international standards. For software life cycles, the three publish one joint standard, which you will meet in a minute.
    The standard in one paragraph
    IEEE Std 1074 (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers Standard 1074), in its 2006 edition, is titled IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) Standard for Developing a Software Project Life Cycle Process (official record↗scan). Its scope says that it provides a process for creating a software project life cycle process, and that it is directed primarily at the process architect of a given project. The 1997 edition organised the work in its Annex A as seventeen activity groups gathered in five groups — the five groups we use today.
    Definitionprocess architect — The person who designs a project's life-cycle process: which activities the project needs, in which order, with which methods, templates and tools.
    A professional checks the status of a standard before citing it, so here it is. IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) lists the 2006 edition as superseded, by ISO/IEC TR 24774:2010 (Technical Report 24774 of the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission: guidelines for process description), a guide for describing processes, which was itself replaced in 2021 (official page↗scan). The software life-cycle processes themselves are defined today in ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207 (joint standard 12207 of the International Organization for Standardization, the International Electrotechnical Commission and the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers: software life cycle processes), whose 2026 edition replaced the 2017 edition on April 29, 2026 (official page↗scan). We still study the 1074 groups because they state the idea of this lesson more plainly than any other document: first list every activity a project may need, then choose and order them.

    Integral activities and umbrella activities
    The integral group should look familiar. It is the standard's version of what Lesson 1 called umbrella activities. Evaluation covers technical reviews and testing; software configuration management has the same name in both lists; documentation development is work product preparation. Several textbooks, and our course schedule, also call this group support activities; the name does not change the content. Two vocabularies, one observation: some work belongs to no single phase, so it must be planned for the whole project.
    The standard gives the checklist; the model gives the order
    Now the most important sentence of this lesson, so write it down. The standard gives you the checklist; the model gives you the order. IEEE Std 1074 (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers Standard 1074) does not say whether design comes before or after the first delivery, or how many times it is repeated. It says that design must exist. The process architect maps the activity groups onto a software life cycle model — waterfall, spiral or any other — and adds the organisation's own methods and templates. The result is the project's life-cycle process.
    Definitionsoftware project life cycle process — The process one project actually follows: the activities chosen from a standard, placed in the order of the chosen life-cycle model, with the organisation's methods, templates and tools attached. IEEE Std 1074 (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers Standard 1074) abbreviates it SPLCP (Software Project Life Cycle Process).
    Our course is a worked example. Block 1, the charter, is project initiation. Block 2 explored the concept: stakeholders, harms, data. Block 3, today, is project planning: the process decision, the life-cycle map, the estimate. Block 5 is requirements, Block 6 design, Block 7 implementation and evaluation. Block 10 is configuration management and documentation planning. Block 12 is evaluation and documentation, and the user guide prepares training. Block 13 is the installation of release 1.0 and maintenance: a change request and a maintenance plan.
    One activity group appears nowhere in the course: retirement. On January 20, what happens to the data your users entered? The standard asks the question; your team will answer it.       What 'prescriptive' means
      Definitionprescriptive process model — A process model that prescribes a set of process elements — framework activities, actions, tasks, work products, quality-assurance and change-control mechanisms — and a workflow, the way these elements relate to one another.
      Prescriptive means that the model tells the team what to do and in which order. Six models in ten minutes. For each one you get three things: what it is, when it fits, and what it costs you. I will draw three of them on the board and show you the others in the figure; copy the drawings, because they are the fastest revision notes you will have.
      Waterfall
      Definitionwaterfall model — A linear, sequential model, also called the classic life cycle: communication, planning, modeling, construction and deployment are performed one after the other, and each phase is completed and approved before the next one begins.
      It fits when the requirements are well understood and stable — for example, adapting a payroll system to a tax rule fixed by law, or moving an existing system to a new platform without changing what it does. Its drawbacks are well documented. Real projects rarely follow a strict sequence. Clients can rarely state every requirement at the start. No working version exists until late in the project, so an error in the requirements is found when it is most expensive to correct. And team members often wait for others to finish, a situation the textbook calls blocking states.
      ↓ The figure of this lesson illustrates this part: Figure 3.
      V-model
      DefinitionV-model — A variation of the waterfall model that pairs every development step with a test level. Going down the left side of the V, the team writes the requirements, the architectural design, the component design and the code; going up the right side, it runs unit, integration, system and acceptance tests. Each test level verifies the step at the same height on the left.
      The pairs used in your textbook are: requirements with acceptance testing, architectural design with system testing, component design with integration testing, code with unit testing. The important word is planned: the acceptance tests are designed while the requirements are being written, not after the code exists. The V-model fits work in which every requirement must be shown to have passed a test — regulated, contractual or safety-related software. It keeps the waterfall's difficulty with change.
      A Québec case shows why the right side matters. In February 2025 the VGQ (Vérificateur général du Québec, the Auditor General of Québec) (official site↗scan) published its audit of the online-services programme of the SAAQ (Société de l'assurance automobile du Québec, the Québec automobile insurance corporation) (official site↗scan), the programme behind SAAQclic (report↗scan, press release↗scan, both in French). Among its findings: the programme's costs had grown by close to 500 million dollars, and when the go-live was confirmed in January 2023, nearly 20 % of the final integrated tests had not been carried out. In the vocabulary of this lesson, the right side of the V was not finished when the system went live.
      Incremental
      Definitionincremental model — A model that delivers the software as a series of increments. The first increment is the core product, which satisfies the basic requirements; each following increment adds features, planned with the users' feedback on the previous one. Each increment is produced by a linear sequence, and the sequences are staggered in time.
      It fits when a limited version is useful early and the core requirements are clear, or when the team is too small to build everything at once. Its cost: the architecture must be sound from the first increment, so that later increments fit without rewriting the earlier ones. Your Sprint 1 and Sprint 2 are two increments.
      Prototyping
      Definitionprototyping — A model in which the team quickly builds a partial version of the software, a prototype, to clarify the requirements with the stakeholders, evaluates it with them and refines it. A throwaway prototype is discarded once it has answered its questions; an evolutionary prototype is refined into the product.
      It fits when the client knows the general objectives but not the details of input, processing or output, and when the user interface decides whether the product succeeds. It has two documented risks: stakeholders see something that seems to work and ask for it to be delivered as it is, and developers keep the shortcuts they took to build it quickly. The countermeasure is a decision made before the first line of the prototype is written: will it be thrown away?
      Spiral
      Definitionspiral model — A risk-driven process model proposed by Barry Boehm. Development proceeds in loops. In each loop the team determines objectives, alternatives and constraints; evaluates the alternatives and identifies and resolves the risks, often with a prototype; develops and verifies the next version of the product; and plans the next loop. Early loops produce a concept or a prototype; later loops produce increasingly complete versions.
      Its distinguishing feature is that risk decides what happens next: the loop that removes the largest risk comes first. It fits large, expensive, high-risk systems. It requires people able to assess risk, and if a major risk is not identified, the model offers no protection. Boehm's own account of the model, including the misreadings he wanted to correct, is free from the SEI (Software Engineering Institute) at CMU (Carnegie Mellon University) (special report of July 2000↗scan); see also the tribute of his university↗scan.
      Concurrent
      Definitionconcurrent model — A model that represents every software engineering activity as a set of states — for example inactive, under development, awaiting changes, under review, under revision, baselined, done — together with the events that move an activity from one state to another. All activities exist at the same time, each in its own state.
      Example: modeling is done when the client reports an inconsistency in the requirements; that event moves modeling to awaiting changes. The concurrent model applies to every type of software and gives an accurate picture of where each activity stands — the same information that a Kanban board shows, and you will build one next week.

        The Unified Process
        DefinitionUnified Process — A use-case driven, architecture-centric, iterative and incremental software process, described in 1999 by Ivar Jacobson, Grady Booch and James Rumbaugh, the authors of the UML (Unified Modeling Language) (official specification↗scan). Its best-known commercial version, the RUP (Rational Unified Process), came from Rational Software, which IBM (International Business Machines) acquired in 2003.
        The UP (Unified Process) divides a project into four phases, each ending with a milestone:
        • Inception — the business case, the scope, the main use cases and the first list of risks;
        • Elaboration — most of the requirements, and an architecture baseline: a first version of the architecture that actually runs;
        • Construction — the features, built in iterations, each iteration ending with an executable increment;
        • Transition — beta testing, user feedback, training and release.
        Inside every phase the team works in iterations, and inside every iteration it performs several disciplines — requirements, analysis and design, implementation, test, deployment — in proportions that change from phase to phase; the figure of this lesson shows those proportions. Your textbook adds a fifth phase, production, in which the software is used and monitored. One warning about the first video below: its author calls the third phase implementation and describes RUP (Rational Unified Process) as agile; the official names of the phases are the four above.
        ↓ The figure of this lesson illustrates this part: Figure 4.
        Component-based development
        Definitioncomponent-based development — A process that builds applications from prepackaged software components, commercial or open-source, with well-defined interfaces; abbreviated CBD (Component-Based Development).
        The textbook gives its steps: research and evaluate the components available for the application domain; consider how they will be integrated; design an architecture that accommodates them; integrate them; test the whole thoroughly. It saves development time when good components exist, and it adds three duties: check each component's licence, follow its security updates, and accept the design decisions it imposes. It is the systematic form of the software importation activity you met in Lesson 2.
        Formal methods
        Definitionformal methods — Mathematically based techniques for specifying, developing and verifying software. The specification is written in a formal language — logic, set theory — and its properties are proved, or checked mechanically by a model checker, a program that explores every reachable state of a model.
        Formal methods remove ambiguity, incompleteness and inconsistency from a specification, and they find design errors that testing misses: a test tries some cases, while verification covers all the cases within the stated bounds. Their costs are time, training, and specifications that clients cannot easily read. They are used where an error costs lives or very large sums. NASA (National Aeronautics and Space Administration) Langley has a formal methods group (official page↗scan). The B method, through the Atelier B tool (official site↗scan), is used to develop safety-related automatic control systems for metros around the world. And engineers at AWS (Amazon Web Services) reported in 2015 that, since 2011, they had used the TLA+ (Temporal Logic of Actions) specification language (official page↗scan) on ten large systems, and that formal methods found bugs in system designs that no other technique they knew of could find (the 2015 article↗scan).

          Life cycle and process model are not the same thing
          Definitionsoftware life cycle — The evolution of a software product from its conception to its retirement. The international vocabulary of our field, ISO/IEC/IEEE 24765 (joint vocabulary standard 24765 of the International Organization for Standardization, the International Electrotechnical Commission and the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) (official page↗scan), defines a life cycle as the evolution of a system, product, service, project or other human-made entity from conception through retirement.
          DefinitionSDLC (Software Development Life Cycle) — The life cycle described as a sequence of phases — typically planning, requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, deployment, operation and maintenance, and finally retirement.
          Definitionprocess model — The strategy that decides how the life-cycle work is carried out: in which order, how many times, with what overlap and with which checkpoints.
          Students confuse the two every year, so here is a test you can apply. The life cycle is what happens to the product: it is conceived, specified, designed, built, tested, deployed, maintained and retired. The process model is how the team organises that work: once, in sequence (waterfall); several times, on growing parts (incremental); in risk-driven loops (spiral). The same life cycle can be run with any of the models.
          Five questions that choose a model

          What happens when risk is decided without being managed? In 2018 the OAG (Office of the Auditor General of Canada) (official site↗scan) audited the federal Phoenix pay system (Report 1, Spring 2018↗scan). In June 2015, the executives cancelled the planned pilot because of major defects affecting critical functions and problems with system stability; the system was then implemented in two waves, on February 24 and April 21, 2016; and the department could not show that the functions implemented on those dates had been fully tested. The Auditor General's overall conclusion reads: "The Phoenix project was an incomprehensible failure of project management and oversight." A risk-driven loop puts that exact decision — go ahead or not, given the known risks — at the start of every cycle, in writing.
          Numbers corner: Basic COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model)
          A decision about the process also needs numbers: how much work is this, and does it fit the time we have? The oldest public answer, still taught everywhere, is the COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model), published by Barry Boehm in 1981 in his book Software Engineering Economics (official page↗scan), from data on 63 completed projects. Its simplest form, Basic COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model), needs one input: the estimated size of the software in KLOC (thousands of Lines Of Code).
          E = a × KLOC^b        effort, in person-months
D = c × E^d           development time, in months
People = E ÷ D        average number of people working in parallel
          where KLOC (thousands of Lines Of Code) is the estimated size, E the effort in person-months, D the development time in months.

          Definitionperson-month — The work one person does on the project in one month; abbreviated PM (person-month). COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model) counts 152 working hours per person-month, holidays and weekends excluded (COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model) II Model Definition Manual↗scan).
          One example, slowly: SafeCampus, estimated at 3 KLOC (thousands of Lines Of Code), in organic mode. The effort first: 3 raised to the power 1.05 is 3.17; multiplied by 2.4, that gives E ≈ 7.6 person-months. Then the time: 7.6 raised to the power 0.38 is 2.16; multiplied by 2.5, that gives D ≈ 5.4 months. Finally, 7.6 divided by 5.4 gives about 1.4 people working in parallel.
          Read the exponents, because they carry the science. In the effort formula, b is greater than 1: doubling the size more than doubles the effort, because more parts and more people mean more communication and more integration. In the time formula, d is well below 1: development time grows much more slowly than effort, so a project cannot be compressed indefinitely by adding people — Brooks's law from last week, written as a formula.
          Read the limits too. Boehm reported that Basic COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model) estimates fell within a factor of 1.3 of the actual effort only 29 % of the time, and within a factor of 2 — between half and double — only 60 % of the time (University of California, Irvine lecture notes↗scan). Basic COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model) is a first, rough estimate. Intermediate COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model) and COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model) II↗scan refine it with cost drivers such as team capability and product complexity.

          The gap sentence
          Now compare with reality, as the Studio asks. The course gives you 14 weeks, about 3.2 months. A team of four, each working the 220 minutes per week of the Studio and the Home Sprint, has about 205 hours, which is 1.35 person-months. COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model) says SafeCampus needs 7.6 person-months over 5.4 months: the gap is a factor of more than five. Inverted, the formula says that 1.35 person-months covers about 580 lines of code.
          Your Block 3 needs one sentence about that gap, and the sentence must say what you conclude. Two honest conclusions exist. We cut the scope to the stories that fit. Or: a model calibrated on professional teams of the 1970s does not describe a part-time student team, and here is the evidence we will collect to measure our real pace. Either one, written with the numbers, is engineering. Ignoring the gap is not.

            Glossary — every term defined in this class
            actionLesson 1 ↗
            A set of tasks that produces a major work product — for example, architectural design, which produces an architecture model.
            activityLesson 1 ↗
            Work aimed at a broad objective — for example, communication with stakeholders — that applies to every project, whatever its domain, size or complexity.
            component-based developmentLesson 4 ↗
            A process that builds applications from prepackaged software components, commercial or open-source, with well-defined interfaces; abbreviated CBD (Component-Based Development).
            concurrent modelLesson 3 ↗
            A model that represents every software engineering activity as a set of states — for example inactive, under development, awaiting changes, under review, under revision, baselined, done — together with the events that move an activity from one state to another. All activities exist at the same time, each in its own state.
            formal methodsLesson 4 ↗
            Mathematically based techniques for specifying, developing and verifying software. The specification is written in a formal language — logic, set theory — and its properties are proved, or checked mechanically by a model checker, a program that explores every reachable state of a model.
            incremental modelLesson 3 ↗
            A model that delivers the software as a series of increments. The first increment is the core product, which satisfies the basic requirements; each following increment adds features, planned with the users' feedback on the previous one. Each increment is produced by a linear sequence, and the sequences are staggered in time.
            person-monthLesson 5 ↗
            The work one person does on the project in one month; abbreviated PM (person-month). COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model) counts 152 working hours per person-month, holidays and weekends excluded (COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model) II Model Definition Manual↗scan).
            prescriptive process modelLesson 3 ↗
            A process model that prescribes a set of process elements — framework activities, actions, tasks, work products, quality-assurance and change-control mechanisms — and a workflow, the way these elements relate to one another.
            process architectLesson 2 ↗
            The person who designs a project's life-cycle process: which activities the project needs, in which order, with which methods, templates and tools.
            process flowLesson 1 ↗
            The way the framework activities, and the actions and tasks inside them, are organised in sequence and in time.
            process frameworkLesson 1 ↗
            The foundation of a complete software process: a small set of framework activities that apply to every software project, together with the umbrella activities that apply across them.
            process modelLesson 5 ↗
            The strategy that decides how the life-cycle work is carried out: in which order, how many times, with what overlap and with which checkpoints.
            process patternLesson 1 ↗
            A description of a process-related problem met during software work, of the environment in which the problem occurs, and of one or more proven solutions to it.
            prototypingLesson 3 ↗
            A model in which the team quickly builds a partial version of the software, a prototype, to clarify the requirements with the stakeholders, evaluates it with them and refines it. A throwaway prototype is discarded once it has answered its questions; an evolutionary prototype is refined into the product.
            retirementLesson 2 ↗
            The planned end of a system's life: users are informed, data is moved or deleted, and the system is switched off.
            SDLC (Software Development Life Cycle)Lesson 5 ↗
            The life cycle described as a sequence of phases — typically planning, requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, deployment, operation and maintenance, and finally retirement.
            software importationLesson 2 ↗
            Deciding which existing software — a library, a component, a service — will be reused instead of written, and how it will be brought into the project.
            software life cycleLesson 5 ↗
            The evolution of a software product from its conception to its retirement. The international vocabulary of our field, ISO/IEC/IEEE 24765 (joint vocabulary standard 24765 of the International Organization for Standardization, the International Electrotechnical Commission and the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) (official page↗scan), defines a life cycle as the evolution of a system, product, service, project or other human-made entity from conception through retirement.
            software processLesson 1 ↗
            A collection of activities, actions and tasks performed when a work product is to be created. The team adapts it: it selects the actions and tasks that suit the project, its people and its deadline.
            software project life cycle processLesson 2 ↗
            The process one project actually follows: the activities chosen from a standard, placed in the order of the chosen life-cycle model, with the organisation's methods, templates and tools attached. IEEE Std 1074 (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers Standard 1074) abbreviates it SPLCP (Software Project Life Cycle Process).
            spiral modelLesson 3 ↗
            A risk-driven process model proposed by Barry Boehm. Development proceeds in loops. In each loop the team determines objectives, alternatives and constraints; evaluates the alternatives and identifies and resolves the risks, often with a prototype; develops and verifies the next version of the product; and plans the next loop. Early loops produce a concept or a prototype; later loops produce increasingly complete versions.
            standardLesson 2 ↗
            A document established by consensus and approved by a recognised body that provides, for common and repeated use, rules, guidelines or characteristics for activities or their results.
            system allocationLesson 2 ↗
            Deciding which functions the software will perform and which will stay with hardware or with people. Example: ClubLend leaves the identity check at the lending desk to a person.
            taskLesson 1 ↗
            A small, well-defined piece of work with a tangible outcome — for example, running one unit test.
            umbrella activitiesLesson 1 ↗
            Activities applied throughout the whole project, across all the framework activities, to manage and control progress, quality, change and risk.
            Unified ProcessLesson 4 ↗
            A use-case driven, architecture-centric, iterative and incremental software process, described in 1999 by Ivar Jacobson, Grady Booch and James Rumbaugh, the authors of the UML (Unified Modeling Language) (official specification↗scan). Its best-known commercial version, the RUP (Rational Unified Process), came from Rational Software, which IBM (International Business Machines) acquired in 2003.
            V-modelLesson 3 ↗
            A variation of the waterfall model that pairs every development step with a test level. Going down the left side of the V, the team writes the requirements, the architectural design, the component design and the code; going up the right side, it runs unit, integration, system and acceptance tests. Each test level verifies the step at the same height on the left.
            waterfall modelLesson 3 ↗
            A linear, sequential model, also called the classic life cycle: communication, planning, modeling, construction and deployment are performed one after the other, and each phase is completed and approved before the next one begins.
            work productLesson 1 ↗
            Anything the process produces and keeps: a document, a model, code, test data, a release.
